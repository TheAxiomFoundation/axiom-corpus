512-2.3 Excluded Trusts for Disabled Individuals Established on or after August 11, 1993

Effective Date: February 1, 2025

Previous Policy

 

Definition

 

An excluded trust is a special type of trust in which assets of a disabled individual can be placed and they do not count as available resources for Medicaid eligibility.  The assets must be used for the sole benefit of the disabled individual during the individual's lifetime.  There are two types of trusts that can be established in Utah to protect the assets of disabled individuals.  These trusts must meet specific criteria to be excluded from countable resources.

This section refers to these trusts as Special Needs Trusts and Pooled Trusts.

Special Needs Trust

 

A Special Needs Trust is an individual trust for a disabled individual under age 65 that meets specific criteria.  If both the individual and the spouse are disabled, the trust could be established for both of them.

Exclude from resources the assets placed in a Special Needs trust when the trust meets all of the following criteria:

The trust is established for an individual who meets the Social Security criteria for being disabled (Social Security or State MRB decision.)

The trust is established for the sole benefit of the disabled individual by the individual, a parent, grandparent, legal guardian of the individual or the court.

Trusts established by the disabled individual must be created on or after December 13, 2016.

The disabled individual is under age 65 when the trust is established and when the assets are transferred to the trust.

The trust contains the assets of the disabled individual, or assets that would have become the individual's assets but for actions to place them directly into the trust.

The trust is irrevocable, meaning that no one can amend, revoke or terminate the trust except that the trust may contain language that says the trust may be amended but only if necessary to conform with subsequent changes to federal or state requirements.

The trust terminates upon the death of the disabled individual or exhaustion of the trust corpus and specifically states that upon the death of the disabled individual, the trustees will notify the State Medicaid agency and pay all amounts remaining in the trust to the State up to the total medical assistance the State has paid on behalf of the disabled individual.

Assets Placed in the Trust.

Assets placed in a Special Needs trust must be the individual's assets.  This includes any assets that would have been payable to the individual such as funds from a settlement, judgment or insurance claim, inheritances that would have been payable to the individual, lump sum payments from various sources, etc.  

If the trust is funded, in whole or in part, with an annuity or structured settlement arrangement that will make periodic payments to the trust, then the controlling documents for such annuities or structured settlement agreements must name the State Medicaid agency as the preferred remainder beneficiary for all guaranteed payments.  A secondary beneficiary may be named to receive any funds that remain after full repayment to the Medicaid agency.

Treat assets placed in a Special Needs trust from a third party as gifted to the disabled individual.  They become part of the corpus of the trust and will be subject to all of these rules governing Special Needs trusts, including the requirement that they will be used to repay the State Medicaid agency.

Verify the assets held in the trust with a schedule, account records or an inventory showing all assets transferred to the trust.  Individuals must report subsequent additions to the trust within 10 days of the date the addition is made. If the 10th day is a non-business day, the individual must report the addition by the business day following the 10th day.  The trustees must provide an updated schedule, account records and inventory of trust assets as requested by the Medicaid agency or at least annually.

Continue to exclude assets held in a Special Needs trust after the disabled individual reaches age 65.  However, treat any assets placed in a Special Needs trust after the individual reaches age 65 as a transfer of assets for less than fair market value (575-1).

Distributions from the Trust.

The sole lifetime beneficiary of the trust is the disabled individual, and the State is the preferred remainder beneficiary.  All distributions must be made to or for the benefit of the disabled individual.  A trust that provides for funds to be used to benefit someone other than the disabled individual is not an excluded Special Needs trust.

Individuals are required to report any payments they will or do receive from the trust on a regular basis.  Infrequent and irregular payments received from the trust must be reported within 10 days of receipt. If the 10th day is a non-business day, the individual must report the payments by the business day following the 10th day.

The trustees must provide an accounting of a disabled individual's account to the individual or the individual's representative to send to the Medicaid agency of trust income and expenditures upon request of the Medicaid agency and when a trustee resigns, but no less often than annually (512-2.3 (E) and 512-2.4).

Trust Cannot be Converted or Create Other Trusts.

A Special Needs trust cannot be converted from an individual Special Needs trust to a Pooled trust defined under 42 U.S.C. Sec. 1396p(d)(4)(C).

Additional trusts cannot be created within the Special Needs trust.

Trustees and Guardians.

A Special Needs trust can be managed by either professional or non-professional trustees.  A professional trustee may charge reasonable fees for managing the trust. Trust funds may be used to refund expenses a non-professional trustee incurs in the normal management of the trust.  

If the disabled individual has a legal guardian, the trust may compensate the guardian as provided by law.  A parent of a minor child may not be compensated from the trust for being the child's guardian.

Pooled Trusts

A Pooled Trust is a type of trust for disabled individuals of any age that meets specific criteria.

Exclude from resources the assets placed in a Pooled Trust for Disabled Individuals when the trust and the individual contractual or "joinder" agreements meet all of the following criteria:

The Pooled Trust is established and managed by a non-profit entity that has been granted that status by the Internal Revenue Service.

The Pooled Trust establishes separate accounts to be used solely for the benefit of disabled persons meeting the Social Security disability criteria.  

An individual applying for or receiving Medicaid who transfers assets to a Pooled Trust must have a disability decision from Social Security or the State Medicaid Review Board.

Accounts must be set up by a parent, grandparent or legal guardian of the individual, the court, or the individual.

The trust must set up individual accounts for each disabled individual who transfers assets to the trust, but funds may be pooled for the purpose of investment.

The trust and the individual accounts must be irrevocable.

The trust must state that upon the death of the disabled trust account beneficiary, the trustees will notify the State Medicaid agency and will pay all amounts remaining in the beneficiary's account to the State up to the total medical assistance paid on behalf of the beneficiary.  

A Pooled Trust may retain a portion of the funds remaining in the deceased trust beneficiary's account not to exceed 50% of the remaining funds to be used for other disabled individuals, but only if the trust has established provisions by which they will assure that the funds are used only for individuals meeting Social Security disability criteria.

A Pooled Trust that retains some portion of the funds upon the death of a disabled account beneficiary must describe how the trust will use such retained funds.  Retained funds placed in other disabled beneficiaries' accounts or used to establish an account for a disabled individual under the trust become part of the balance of such individuals' accounts and are subject to the Medicaid repayment requirement.  

The trust may include a process of providing one-time, incidental services or benefits for qualified disabled persons who do not have accounts under the pooled trust.

Each individual who chooses to place assets in a Pooled Trust must complete an individual written agreement with the trustees.  Sometimes these written agreements are called Joinder Agreements.  The Joinder agreement must include:

the name and age of the disabled individual

a schedule of the assets to be placed in the account

the date of the agreement

the name and relationship of the individual establishing the account (if different than the individual)

a description of how funds from the beneficiary's account will be disbursed upon death (for example, all will be repaid to Medicaid; some portion not to exceed 50% will be retained by the trust, etc.)

It may also contain additional information such as:

a guardian's or representative's name, address, and phone number, if applicable

a secondary remainder beneficiary who could receive funds in the event of a full repayment of the State Medicaid agency

other important information as determined by the trustees.

The trust accounts cannot be liquidated prior to the death of the individual without first repaying the State Medicaid agency.

The disabled individual cannot control any spending by the trust.

Assets Placed in a Pooled Trust.

Assets placed in a Pooled Trust must be the individual's assets.  This includes any assets that would have been payable to the individual such as funds from a settlement, judgment or insurance claim, inheritances that would have been payable to the individual, lump sum payments from various sources, etc.  

If the trust is funded, in whole or in part, with an annuity or structured settlement arrangement that will make periodic payments to the trust, then the controlling documents for such annuities or structured settlement agreements must name the State Medicaid agency as the preferred remainder beneficiary for all guaranteed payments.  A secondary beneficiary may be named to receive any funds that remain after full repayment to the Medicaid agency. (If the Pooled Trust retains part of the funds and Medicaid only receives partial repayment, then no funds can be given to a secondary remainder beneficiary.)

A disabled individual may be any age when his or her funds are transferred to a Pooled Trust account.  The resources transferred to the Pooled Trust will be excluded from resources for Medicaid eligibility.  However, if an individual age 65 or older transfers any funds to a Pooled Trust, such transfer will be treated as a transfer of resources for less than fair market value and could result in a penalty period preventing the individual from receiving long-term care services under Medicaid.

Assets placed in a Pooled Trust from a third party will be treated as gifted to the disabled individual.  They will become part of the balance of the disabled individual's account in the Pooled Trust and will be subject to all of these rules governing Pooled Trusts, including the requirement that they will be used to repay the State Medicaid agency.

The trustees must provide a schedule, account records or an inventory of all assets transferred to the trust.  Subsequent additions to the trust must be reported to the State Medicaid agency within 10 days.  If the 10th day is a non-business day, the individual must report the subsequent addition by the business day following the 10th day. The trustees will provide an updated schedule, account records and inventory of trust assets as requested by the Medicaid agency or at least annually.

Distributions from the Trust.

The sole lifetime beneficiary of the trust is the disabled individual, and the State is the preferred remainder beneficiary.  All distributions must be made to or for the benefit of the disabled individual.  A trust that provides for funds to be used to benefit someone other than the disabled individual is not an excluded Pooled trust.

Individuals are required to report any payments they will or do receive from the trust on a regular basis.  Infrequent and irregular payments received from the trust must be reported within 10 days of receipt.

The trustees must provide an accounting of a disabled individual's account to the individual or the individual's representative to send to the Medicaid agency of trust income and expenditures upon request of the Medicaid agency and when a trustee resigns, but no less often than annually (512-2).

Trustees and Guardians.

The trustees of the Pooled Trust may charge reasonable fees for managing the trust.  

If the disabled individual has a legal guardian, the trust may compensate the guardian as provided by law.  A parent of a minor child may not be compensated from the trust for being the child's guardian.

Transfers of Assets and Excluded Trusts

Do not treat assets of a disabled individual that are placed in a special needs trust while the individual is under age 65 as a transfer of assets for less than fair market value.  

Assets that will be received over time, such as payments from a structured settlement payment arrangement that makes periodic payments directly to the trust must guarantee that all payments will be paid to the trust during the disabled individual's lifetime and must name the State Medicaid agency as the preferred remainder beneficiary for all guaranteed payments upon the individual's death up to the amount Medicaid has paid in benefits or services on behalf of the individual, to avoid being treated as a transfer of assets.

A transfer of assets has occurred if something in the trust prevents the funds from being used for the sole benefit of the individual or allows funds to be used for someone else.

Treat payments of trust funds as a transfer for less than fair market value when the funds are used for a purpose other than to benefit the disabled individual.

Treat any assets transferred to a either a Special Needs trust or a Pooled Trust when the individual is age 65 or older as a transfer of assets for less than fair market value (575).

If a trust is funded with an annuity or other structured payment arrangement, any provision or action that does or will divert payments or principal from such annuity or payment arrangement to someone other than the excluded trust or the State Medicaid agency is treated as a transfer of assets for less than fair market value, with the exception that the agreement can name a secondary remainder beneficiary to receive any funds remaining after full repayment to the Medicaid agency.

Trust Payments

Expenditures the trustee makes from an excluded Special Needs trust or from a Pooled Trust must be for the sole benefit of the disabled individual.  The following provisions apply to payments from both Special Needs trusts and Pooled Trusts.

Count as income any payments to the disabled individual, or to a guardian or representative payee for the individual, in the month the payment is made.  However, a trustee may designate the guardian or representative to make specific types of purchases for the benefit of the disabled individual and provide the funds to the guardian or representative to make such purchases.  The trustee may also arrange to refund the guardian or representative for the cost of specific types of purchases made for the benefit of the disabled individual.

Count as in-kind income payments that cover the cost of shelter expenses for the disabled individual.  These are subject to the Presumed Maximum Value rules found in 415-10.

If any payments from the trust provide an ongoing and continuing benefit to other persons, those other persons must contribute a pro-rata share to the trust for the expenses associated with their use of the acquisition.  (For example, if the trust pays the expenses of a vehicle that a family member uses to transport the disabled individual, but that family member also uses it for other personal transportation needs not related to transporting the disabled individual, the family member needs to track mileage used for other purposes and pay a mileage cost back to the trust.)

Contracts to provide personal services to the disabled individual must be in writing and executed in advance of any services being provided and paid.  The contract must describe the services to be provided, when or how often services are to be provided, who will provide the services, when payments will be made and the rate of pay which must be a fair market rate consistent with community rates for the type and quality of the services to be provided.  If the care giver is a member of the disabled individual's family, a doctor's statement may be required to assure that the family member has the skills and ability to provide the services.  When a contract does not exist prior to the services being provided and paid, treat such transactions as a transfer for less than fair market value.

The following list is an example of some types of payments that can be made for the benefit of the disabled individual from an excluded trust that do not count as income and do not result in a transfer of assets penalty.  This list is not intended to be all inclusive.  Other types of payments are possible.

Medical services, adaptive equipment, prosthetics, and durable medical equipment for the individual not covered by Medicaid

Special therapies, treatments, or medical services for the individual whether or not all such services are considered medically necessary

Personal attendant services the individual needs to perform daily activities or to participate in recreational activities including vacations.  (The need for an attendant must be due to the individual's medical or mental health needs.  A doctor's statement may be needed.)

Respite care services

Vacation costs of the individual (but not for others who accompany the individual other than a required personal attendant)

Taxes, fees and expenses for managing the trust.  (These must be reasonably related to the actual services provided by the trustee.)

Personal items the individual needs or wants that may include clothing, entertainment equipment, personal belongings, etc.

Modifications to a home or residence where the individual lives.  (If the individual's care givers own the home, the modifications must be required due to the individual's disabling condition.)

Purchase of a pre-need irrevocable funeral contract for the individual before the individual's death

Health insurance premiums for the individual

Other expenditures are possible but may need to include an explanation of how they provide a benefit to the individual

Treat payments made to others that are not for the benefit of the disabled individual as a transfer of assets for less than fair market value.

See Sec. 512-2.4 for Annual Accounting Requirements.

Income Only Trusts

 

Federal statute allows individuals to establish an "income only" trust, sometimes called "Miller Trusts," in certain states that do not provide Medicaid for Medically Needy institutionalized individuals.  Utah provides nursing facility and other long-term care coverage to Medically Needy institutionalized individuals.  (This group is typically referred to as the "Spenddown" group or program.)  Therefore, an "income only" trust is not an excluded trust.  Income transferred to such a trust would be treated as a transfer for less than fair market value and subject the individual to a possible penalty period.  Follow the procedures for referring a trust for review if you believe a trust is an "income only" trust.
