1003 Preadmission Screening Criteria for an Applicant or Member who is Elderly or Physically Disabled (EPD)

 

 

Revised 06/09/2026

Policy

The PAS tool for an applicant or member who is EPD includes the following categories:

  • Intake information: The assessor collects basic information about the customer, which includes:

    • PAS date and time

    • Age

    • Birthdate

    • Gender

    • Location at time of assessment

    • Telephone number

    • DD Status (Not DD; Potential DD; DD in NF; and DD)

    • Authorized Representative and phone number

    • Physical measurements, and hospitalization history.

NOTE     This information does not count towards the PAS score.

 

  • Functional assessment: The assessor collects information on an the customer’s need for assistance with activities of daily living in the residential environment or other routine setting, including:

    • Mobility;

    • Transferring;

    • Bathing;

    • Dressing;

    • Grooming;

    • Eating;

    • Toileting;

    • Continence, including bowel and bladder functioning; and

    • Communication and sensory skills, including hearing, expressive communication, and vision

 

  • Emotional and Cognitive assessment: The Assessor collects information regarding the emotional and cognitive functions of the customer’s:

    • Orientation to person, place, and time. The caregiver’s judgement is also taken into account; and

    • Behaviors, including:

      • Wandering;

      • Self-injurious behavior;

      • Aggression;

      • Resistiveness; and

      • Disruptive behavior.

 

  • Medical assessment: The assessor collects the following information regarding the medical issues of the customer:

    • Medical conditions that have an impact on the functional ability in relation to activities of daily living, continence, and vision;

    • Medical conditions that require medical or nursing services and treatment;

    • Medications, treatments, and allergies; and

    • Specific services and treatments currently being received.

 

A copy of the PAS tool is available from AHCCCS upon request. The Administration uses the Assessor’s PAS assessment to calculate three scores: a functional score, a medical score, and a total score.

 

Functional score

  • The functional score comes from answers in the functional, emotional, and cognitive assessment sections.

  • Each answer has a point value. That point value is multiplied by a weight. This creates a weighted score for each answer.

  • The following areas are scored using the table shown under Functional Scoring Table:

    • Activities of daily living

    • Continence

    • Sensory

    • Orientation

    • Behavior

  • The weighted scores are added together to get the functional score.

  • Each item can have a weighted score between 0 and 15.

  • The highest possible functional score a person can get is 166.

 

Medical score

  • Items are scored based on:

    • Medical conditions listed under the Medical Scoring Table

    • Medical or nursing services or treatments under the Medical Scoring Table

  • The medical score is based on whether the person has:

    • A major diagnosis of Alzheimer’s, Dementia, or Organic Brain Syndrome (OBS)

    • A major diagnosis of Paralysis

    • A current need for oxygen

  • The highest medical score a person can get is 31.5

 

Total score

  • The total score is the functional score plus the medical score

  • For a person who is EPD, the required minimum score is 60

  • A person is considered at immediate risk of institutionalization if their total score is 60 or higher

 

The tables below show how many points each item can get and how much each item is worth.

  • Functional assessment points: 

    • A score of zero means the person has little or no trouble doing the activity.

    • The highest score means the person has a lot of difficulty or serious impairment. 

  • Medical assessment points: 

    • A score of zero means the person:

      • May have a diagnosis of Alzheimer’s, Dementia, or OBS but does not meet the criteria to score as a major diagnosis. 

      • May have a diagnosis of Paralysis but does not meet the criteria to score as a major diagnosis. 

      • Does not have a current need for oxygen.

 

NOTE     Criteria for when to make Alzheimer’s, Dementia, or OBS a major diagnosis can be found in the EPD Appendix under Medical Conditions, Neurological. Criteria for when to make Paralysis a major diagnosis can be found under Medical Conditions, Musculoskeletal.

Definitions

Term

Definition

Aggression

Physically attacking another, including:

  • Throwing objects at a person

  • Punching

  • Biting

  • Pushing

  • Pinching

  • Pulling hair

  • Scratching

  • Physically threatening behavior

  • Destroying property as a result of trying to hurt another person

Bathing

The process of washing, rinsing, and drying all parts of the body, including a person’s ability to transfer in and out of a tub or shower and set-up including gathering equipment, running the water, and setting the temperature. Bathing also includes, taking sponge baths for the purpose of maintaining adequate hygiene and skin integrity.

Continence

The ability to voluntarily control the discharge of body waste from bladder and bowel.

Current or currently

Belonging to the present time.

Dressing

A person’s ability to dress and undress, which includes choosing and putting on clean clothes and footwear, including assistive devices such as prostheses, braces, and anti-embolism stockings. It also includes the ability to choose appropriate clothing for the weather and ability to fasten buttons and zippers. It does not include choosing clothing based on style or appearance. 

Eating

The ability to eat and drink. This includes picking up the food, bringing it to your mouth, chewing it, and swallowing it. It does not include cooking the food – only the act of feeding oneself. 

Emotional and cognitive functioning

A person’s orientation and mental state, as evidenced in aggressive, self-injurious, wandering, disruptive, and resistive behaviors.

Functional assessment

An evaluation of information about a person’s ability to perform activities related to:

  • Activities of daily living;

  • Communication and sensory patterns; and

  • Behaviors

Grooming

The process of tending to one's appearance. Grooming includes:

  • Combing or brushing hair;

  • Shaving; and

  • Oral care (including denture care).

Grooming does not include aesthetics such as styling, skin care, nail care, and applying cosmetics.

Intervention for behavior

Treatment to control the behavior. Intervention may be formal or informal, and includes actions taken by a caregiver to control the behavior.

Some examples include, but are not limited to:

  • Verbally redirecting

  • Physically stopping the behavior

  • Interventions outlined in a Behavior Treatment or Modification Plan

  • Chemical restraints

Medical assessment

An evaluation of a customer's medical condition and the customer’s need for medical services.

Medical or nursing services and treatments, or services and treatments

Specific, ongoing medical, psychiatric, or nursing intervention used actively to resolve or prevent deterioration of a medical condition. Durable medical equipment and activities of daily living assistive devices are not treatment unless the equipment or device is used specifically and actively to resolve the existing medical condition.

Mobility

The extent of a customer’s purposeful movement within a residential environment.

Orientation

A person’s awareness of their environment in relation to person, place, and time.

Physically disabled

A customer who is determined physically impaired by the Administration through the PAS assessment as allowed under the Administration’s Section 1115 Waiver with CMS.

Resistiveness

Inappropriately stubborn and uncooperative behaviors, including:

  • Passive or obvious behaviors

  • Obstinance

  • Unwillingness to participate in self-care or take necessary medications

Resistiveness does not include difficulties with auditory processing or reasonable expressions of self-advocacy.

Self-injurious behavior

Repeated self-induced, abusive behavior that is directed toward infliction of immediate physical harm to the body.

Self-injurious behavior does not include suicide attempts, accidents or risky lifestyle choices.

Sensory

Of or relating to the senses.

Toileting

A person’s ability to empty their bowel and bladder in the appropriate places. It includes all of the following:

  • Using the toilet, bedpan, or urinal

  • Transferring on and off the toilet

  • Wiping

  • Washing hands

  • Changing protective garments

  • Managing a colostomy bag or catheter

  • Adjusting clothing

  • Flushing the toilet

Transferring

A person's ability to move between two surfaces within a residential environment. Transferring includes getting into and out of a chair, sofa, or bed, but does not include transfers to a toilet, bath, or shower

Vision

The ability to perceive objects with the eyes.

Wandering

The behavior of moving about with no rational purpose as a result of disorientation or memory problems that puts the safety of the person or others at risk. The person tends to wander beyond the borders of their environment or to areas within the environment that pose a safety risk.

 

Proof

Proof of immediate risk of institutionalization shall be demonstrated through:

  • Customer score equal to or higher than the applicable PAS threshold score; or

  • Finding by a physician consultant reviewer that the customer has this status.
